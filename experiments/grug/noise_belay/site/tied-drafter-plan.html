A tied drafter for speculative pretraining

Test whether a cheap subnetwork, derived from the current large model, can predict which parameter updates will help that large model. Keep a fixed correspondence between their weights, lift draft proposals into the large model, and verify them on fresh data. The first result should be update-ranking accuracy and total verification cost. A complete replacement training system comes only after that result supports it.

The target, called the hero, is Marin Belay candidate 11: 1,026,488,855 parameters, six layers, width 512, a mixture of KDA (a recurrent attention mechanism) and global attention, and 512 routed experts (feedforward modules selected per token), with eight selected per token. Shared experts run for every token. Latent projections compress the inputs to those experts. Its reference uses a global batch of 128 × 4,096 tokens. This new experiment is distinct from the fixed-recipe Noise port.

1. The hypothesis

Shared weights may make the draft a more faithful predictor of update effects than an independently initialized small model. Similarity of ordinary loss curves is insufficient: the draft must predict the sign and relative magnitude of loss changes caused by specific proposed interventions. Shared coordinates solve the bookkeeping problem of where an update goes; they do not guarantee that it remains beneficial when omitted computation is restored.

MatFormer provides the architectural precedent: nested feedforward networks can share weights inside a transformer. Its evidence concerns useful models at multiple inference sizes, not replacing hero optimization with small-model updates. Once-for-All is a broader precedent for jointly trained subnetworks. Both motivate an experiment; neither establishes this training speedup.

If feedforward nesting cannot meet the intended cost, add a separately named architecture ablation that shrinks attention dimensions or residual width. Do not call that model otherwise identical. Quantization is a later ablation: fewer bits can reduce memory and improve supported kernels, but does not itself give one-eighth as many weights or operations.

3. Parameter correspondence and initialization

Let R select the draft coordinates from hero weights θ, and let P insert a draft update into those coordinates, with zeros elsewhere. For the selection-only version, R P is the identity on the draft coordinates. The proposal is:

φ = Rθ    →    propose Δφ    →    θ′ = θ + α PΔφ

Keeping the same router and top-eight rule does not freeze the selected expert identities: earlier draft activations can change later routing decisions. Record route disagreement separately. A frozen-route ablation may diagnose that effect, but must charge the hero forward used to supply those routes. Keep a machine-readable map of every selected dimension, scale and parameter path. Refresh φ from the current θ before each proposal episode. Build proposals in scratch buffers; sharing logical coordinates must not mutate the live hero before verification. Retain hero optimizer state unchanged for rejected proposals. In the initial ranking and persistence experiment, accepted proposals change only parameters: preserve the hero’s moments and step counter, then resume ordinary updates. This makes continuation comparisons differ only in their starting parameters. Alternative moment resets or transfers are later ablations, with their costs reported.

Start with deterministic nested subsets of the hero’s existing weights and no change to the established hero initialization. Dropping units changes output variance and function. Compare unscaled slicing against a fixed, training-data-calibrated output scale. Fit any scales on a designated calibration split, freeze them before the ranking test, and include calibration cost. Do not assume a universal square-root scaling after nonlinear experts and normalization.

A separate from-scratch arm can use correlated initialization: generate a common random tensor, take nested slices, and apply explicitly specified width-dependent scales. μP motivates consistent scaling across widths, but its hyperparameter-transfer evidence does not guarantee update transfer. Net2Net motivates function-preserving expansion where the architecture permits it. Neither should be retrofitted into the fixed Belay control and presented as the same initialization.

An optional grouped-weight variant can write θ = Pφ + δ, where δ holds the hero’s additional detail. Measure how transfer deteriorates as δ grows. Keeping every update permanently in the range of P restricts the learned update space. Rotating nested subsets or occasional full-model updates are separate treatments for accessing the remaining capacity; each adds cost and changes the hypothesis.

Quantization is an optional second axis, after establishing whether nested weights help. Keep the hero’s master weights unchanged; form the draft as a quantized view of the selected weights with recorded scales and rounding. Matching a seed or quantization bins does not preserve the omitted computation. Measure whether rounding changes candidate rankings or creates a dead zone for small updates. A hybrid gradient-based draft would need an explicit derivative approximation through quantization; a strict forward-only arm must instead measure the actual quantized responses. Charge scale calibration, quantization and synchronization to the proposal cost.

4. Candidate proposals and controls

The cheapest informative first experiment may use ordinary backprop on the small draft to propose updates. Label it hybrid: the hero can skip backward passes, but the system is not globally backprop-free. Evaluate forward-only draft proposals as a separate arm once their local cost is competitive. Also test a cheap predictor that combines recent hero updates; a draft language model need not be the best proposal mechanism.

The first proposal recipe is one Adam step on ordinary next-token cross-entropy using the proposal batch, with draft optimizer state reset consistently for each episode. Generate four step scales (0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2), plus norm-matched extrapolation and no-op controls, within the eight-candidate cap. Predict each candidate’s effect by the difference between draft losses before and after its restricted update on verification tokens; record this prediction before target scoring. Include both draft training and scoring in cost.

The shared pool contains only updates within the same selected coordinate subspace. The full hero update is a separate diagnostic reference. An independent draft must first be trained on the same preceding training-token budget, with that cost recorded; an untrained independent draft is only a negative control. Its predetermined coordinate map intentionally lacks learned feature alignment, so this comparison measures alignment and synchronization together. A calibrated feature-alignment arm would be a separate experiment.

Use identical token batches, loss masks and candidate norm or step-size ranges across relevant arms. At each checkpoint, evaluate a shared candidate pool with each proxy, as well as each arm’s own proposals. The shared pool separates ranking skill from proposal quality. Never select a checkpoint, scale or candidate using final Paloma evaluation.

5. Bounded ranking test

Begin with three hero checkpoints representing early, middle and late training. Use a fixed, disjoint proposal/calibration/verification partition of training data, and reserve separate fresh batches for confirmation. At each checkpoint, use eight batch groups and at most eight candidates per arm. Start with one draft width; add the next width only if ranking or cost could change the decision. These are design caps, not permission to launch every combination automatically.

  1. Measure hero loss at unchanged weights on the verification batch.
  2. Generate candidates using only proposal data and allowed history; record draft time, synchronization, candidate construction and bytes moved.
  3. Record each proxy’s predicted loss change before evaluating hero candidates.
  4. Measure hero loss for each lifted candidate on the same verification tokens. Include failed and rejected proposals in cost.
  5. Confirm the selected update on fresh batches; compare with extrapolation using the same search budget.

Report Spearman rank correlation, pairwise ordering accuracy, precision of predicted improvements, selected-candidate regret against the tested pool, and accepted loss improvement per GPU-second. Show paired confidence intervals over batch groups, stratified by checkpoint and seed. Candidates sharing a batch are not independent samples. Include candidate magnitude and parameter-subspace coverage so near-zero updates cannot appear successful merely by avoiding harm.

Check whether numerical variation is smaller than the loss differences being ranked. Use repeated unchanged-weight evaluations and a controlled FP32 reference on reduced shapes where feasible. BF16 elementwise equality is not the success criterion; nonfinite values, substantial loss drift and routing instability remain failures. On the selection batch require a negative mean loss change whose magnitude exceeds both three sequence-level standard errors and the measured repeatability floor. Confirm the sign independently on fresh batches before acceptance. Estimate uncertainty by sequence or document groups, not independent tokens. Freeze the calibrated noise floor and all thresholds before the held-out ranking experiment. Proceed to persistence only if the paired confidence interval for improvement per total GPU-second favors the strongest extrapolation control; otherwise report the uncertainty or stop.

6. Persistence and full cost

For the best supported variant and its strongest cheap control, continue from identical hero checkpoints for 1, 4 and 16 ordinary training updates after an accepted proposal. Use the same subsequent data and optimizer-state policy. Measure whether the initial gain persists, reverses, or merely borrowed progress from the next ordinary update. Repeat with multiple seeds before a training-speed claim. Short continuation does not estimate an equivalent number of training steps. Only a subsequent full training comparison, reaching a prespecified held-out loss with matched data access, can estimate k or establish time-to-quality gains. Paloma, the reference collection of evaluation corpora, stays outside candidate selection.

The economic condition is that proposal, synchronization, verification, rejection and fallback costs together buy more progress than ordinary optimization. If an episode demonstrably replaces k ordinary updates, its idealized speedup is k times ordinary-update cost divided by the complete episode cost. k must come from sustained matched-quality progress; a large single-step loss drop does not establish it.

Unlike speculative decoding, candidate weight states generally require distinct target evaluations. Parallel evaluation can reduce latency while increasing GPU-hours. Report both. The speculative-decoding guarantee preserves a sampling distribution; an endpoint training-loss test offers no corresponding guarantee about the optimization trajectory.

7. Resources and stopping rules

Use the RNO2A GPU cluster at interactive priority. Start with CPU shape/coordinate checks and one H100 for draft latency where it fits, then one eight-H100 node for target verification. Keep aggregate use within the existing 256-H100 ceiling; that ceiling is not a reservation. Scale only after measured memory or a positive cost-adjusted ranking result justifies it. Keep calibration and bounded ranking within a proposed ten-GPU-hour first-stage cap, reporting any reason that cap needs revision before expanding scope.

Stop expanding the draft if it cannot beat extrapolation at matched total cost, if ranking fails on fresh batches, or if accepted gains disappear in continuation. If fixed attention and head work prevents useful draft savings, report that cost floor. If joint nested-model training is needed to make the draft useful, test it as a new hero-training recipe and charge its extra objectives; do not hide that cost in initialization.

8. Why the current naive Noise port is not the starting implementation

Noise is a forward-only pretraining competition; Boost is its stagewise loss-probing phase. Each of our twelve Boost readouts uses 512 positive/negative direction pairs through a vocabulary loss, reusing cached activations and projections. The existing Belay component measurements are 61.53 ms for a loss-forward, 156.90 ms for forward plus backward, and 61.23 seconds for twelve full-cycle Boost readouts on eight H100s at the full training batch. Removing the measured backward computation saves about 95 ms; the measured readouts cost about 390 times the whole forward/backward operation before parameter reconstruction. With the measured probe cost held fixed, making all remaining treatment work free still cannot produce a per-update speedup at equal progress. This is a conclusion about that naive implementation, not an impossibility theorem for forward-only learning.

The draft proposal must avoid transplanting thousands of expensive readout probes wholesale. Begin with a small candidate pool and measured target-verification cost. The background report and readout timing record preserve the original evidence.

9. Interpreting the 1,000–10,000× claim

The Noise README attributes this claim to Dust’s efficiency relative to EGGROLL, a weight-space evolutionary-search baseline. Dust uses token-local activation perturbations, obtaining many local observations within a sequence and reusing a clean forward. The comparison is not a production wall-clock speedup over backprop. In the published ladder, EGGROLL at population 16k remains worse than Dust at 64. Matching-population estimates in the thousands come from fitting and extrapolating that ladder; they are not completed runs at those populations. See the paper’s setup and results and Appendix D.

The denominator can therefore be extremely expensive while Dust remains costly relative to backprop. Our record must distinguish the measured population comparison, the extrapolated matching cost, the later Noise competition recipe, and our architecture-specific Belay adaptation.
